Security properties of individual protocol entries do not, by themselves,
specify which entries may be processed together. This distinction matters
when a receiver groups several sender-associated inputs into one operation:
an entry may have a legitimate origin while the collection repeats a party
that the interface expects to occur only once. Work on secure-messaging
session handling illustrates the broader need to examine composition
boundaries separately from their components
\cite{cremers2023session}. Here we study a different boundary, inside one
receiver batch rather than between sessions and conversations.

\kwaay is a post-quantum, X3DH-like deniable authenticated key-exchange
protocol \cite{collins2024kwaay}. Its \batchreceive operation groups inputs
under receiver-side state, and its full construction states that the elements
of one call correspond to different parties
\cite[Sec.~5.1, p.~21]{collins2024kwaayfull}. The condition is already part of
the source protocol. Our question is what relation it preserves, and whether
a restriction over a different coordinate could preserve the same relation.
We call the same-batch party relation \distinctparty; this analytical name is
ours, not an additional condition discovered to be missing from K-Waay.

The distinction is concrete. One modeled party can originate two messages in
different sender occurrences. A batch containing those entries can have
different messages and no repeated acceptance of either exact origin, yet
still contain the same party twice. Conversely, supplying one exact entry to
two positions creates a different question about acceptance multiplicity.
Party identity, message identity, and sender-occurrence matching must
therefore be distinguished before deciding what an admission control achieves.
The individual notions of identity and injective correspondence are
established concepts \cite{lupetti2006names,lowe1997hierarchy}; our concern is
their relationship at this particular source-motivated interface.

\paragraph{Questions and approach.}
We study three questions, stated precisely in Section~2: which repeated-party
compositions become reachable when the condition is relaxed (RQ1), whether
one matching sender origin can support two receiver-acceptance occurrences in
that abstraction (RQ2), and whether a message-level restriction is sufficient
for same-batch party distinction (RQ3). We compare three fixed-two-slot
Tamarin models \cite{meier2013tamarin}: relaxed admission, message-level
restriction, and party-level admission. They share sender generation, input
collection, origin matching, and sequential processing. The guarded variants
constrain different coordinates and add their corresponding rejection
branches. The party-level variant is a positive control, not an assertion
that a direct comparison is the only way to maintain the source condition.

\paragraph{Results and contribution.}
The contribution is a formal semantic analysis of the stated condition and a
controlled separation of message-, occurrence-, and party-level properties
under explicit modeling assumptions. The recorded results support three
parts of that argument. Relaxed admission has a trace with one matching Send
and two matching \receiveraccept occurrences in one batch context. The
message-level variant satisfies message distinction and scoped occurrence
injectivity, but still admits and processes two different messages from one
party. Finally, party-level admission constrains the collected pair itself;
the recorded safety result confirms party distinction between accepted
occurrences, and a valid distinct-party batch remains reachable. The middle
result is the central separation: success for message distinction and scoped
injectivity does not entail same-batch party distinction.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The analysis assumes adversarial control over composition at the modeled
boundary. It does not establish that a network attacker controls a deployed
batch builder. Fresh symbolic messages and sender-session coordinates, an
idealized persistent origin relation, and model-local receiver events keep
the comparison small and explicit. We provide a source-to-abstraction mapping
but no refinement theorem for complete K-Waay executions. Thus the results
are not a cryptographic attack, a deployed vulnerability, or a theorem for
arbitrary batch sizes. We report existing model executions; the accompanying
material records exact properties, hashes, and commands but does not contain
raw prover transcripts. These limits qualify both the contribution and its
reproducibility claims.
